Toilet Float Valve Repair for South Plainfield, NJ Homes

A toilet float valve controls how high the tank fills. When it sticks, wears out or is set too high, water keeps running into the overflow tube, the toilet hisses and the water bill creeps up. Many floats can be adjusted in five minutes, and a worn fill valve is a cheap part to replace.

This guide explains how to tell a float problem from a flapper problem, how to adjust or replace the valve and when a plumber is the better choice.

What a float valve does and why it fails

A float valve, also called a fill valve or ballcock, refills the tank after every flush. A float rises with the water and, at a set height, tells the valve to close. If the float is set too high, water spills into the overflow tube and the toilet runs. If the valve itself is worn, it cannot close fully and the toilet hisses or refills on its own.

Over years, sediment and mineral scale clog the small passages inside the valve, and rubber seals harden. Hard water speeds this up. A fill valve is a plastic part that rarely lasts forever, and most homeowners find one needs replacing every ten years or so.

Float problem or flapper problem?

Two parts cause most running toilets. The flapper is the rubber seal at the bottom of the tank, and a worn one lets water leak into the bowl. The float and fill valve are at the top, and a bad one lets water flow into the overflow tube. Telling them apart saves a trip to the hardware store.

How to adjust the float

1

Take the lid off

Set it somewhere safe and look at the fill valve and the water line.

2

Find the adjustment

Most valves have a screw or a clip on the rod or a sliding float cup.

3

Lower the water level

The water should sit about an inch below the top of the overflow tube.

4

Flush and check

Wait for the tank to refill and confirm the water stops below the tube.

If the water still runs after adjustment, the valve may be worn. Shut off the supply, hold the float up and see if the water stops. If it does, the valve seat is likely failing and the whole assembly should be replaced.

Replacing a fill valve step by step

Turn off the water at the shutoff valve behind the toilet and flush to drain the tank. Sponge out the last water. Disconnect the supply line at the bottom of the tank, loosen the locknut under the tank and lift the old valve out.

Set the new valve in the same hole, tighten the locknut by hand and a little more, and reconnect the supply line. Attach the refill tube to the overflow tube, turn the water on slowly and set the height. Watch for leaks at both connections for several minutes.

What water in South Plainfield means for toilet parts

Middlesex Water, which serves portions of South Plainfield, reported average hardness of 157 ppm in its 2025 report, with a range of 90 to 308 ppm. That is about 9 grains per gallon on average, enough to leave scale inside small valve passages over the years.

If your toilet fill valve is older than ten years and clogs often, scale is the likely reason. Replacing it is cheaper than repeated adjustments, and a plumber can also check the supply shutoff, which tends to be corroded in hard-water homes.

Source type: Utility water quality report (Middlesex Water, 2025)

When to call a plumber instead

A fill valve is within reach of a careful homeowner, but there are signs that more is going on. A shutoff valve that will not close, a supply line that leaks at the fitting, a cracked tank or a toilet that rocks on the floor are all reasons to call. Cutting a stuck valve can lead to a flood.

Water on the floor around the base points to a failed wax ring, not the float. Repeated clogs point to a drain problem. If the bill has risen and every toilet checks out, see leak detection, because a meter that moves with everything off means a leak somewhere else.

What a repair visit costs and what it should include

A fill valve swap is one of the less expensive plumbing jobs, and the part is inexpensive. The cost of a visit comes from the call, the time and any extra items you ask for, such as a new flapper or a new shutoff. A plumber should tell you the price before starting and replace the worn parts together so you do not have to call again for the flapper next month.

A good visit ends with a test. The plumber flushes several times, checks the water level, confirms the shutoff closes and looks for drips at every joint. If you live near Edison or Piscataway, the same visit can cover other small repairs in the house.

Standards note

According to the EPA WaterSense program, labeled toilets use 1.28 gallons per flush or less. If your toilet is old and needs more than a valve, a labeled replacement is the standard that saves the most water.
