Dripping Bathroom Faucet Repair in South Plainfield, NJ, NJ

A dripping bathroom faucet is nearly always a worn cartridge, washer or O-ring. Identify the faucet type, shut off the water, replace the worn part and test. Most repairs take under an hour and cost very little in parts.

This guide walks through the steps for the most common faucet types and explains when a plumber is the faster, safer answer.

Identify your faucet type first

The repair depends on the design. A two-handle faucet usually has a washer and seat or a ceramic cartridge for each side. A single-handle faucet has a cartridge, a ball or a disc inside. Look at the handle and the spout, and take a photo before taking anything apart.

Steps to stop the drip

1

Shut off the water

Use the valves under the sink and open the faucet to drain it.

2

Plug the drain

A rag or stopper keeps small parts from falling in.

3

Remove the handle

Pop off the cap, remove the screw and lift the handle.

4

Swap the worn part

Match the new cartridge or washer to the old one.

Reassemble in reverse, turn the water on slowly and open the faucet to purge air. Watch for leaks at the base and the handle. If the drip continues, the seat may be worn or the part may be the wrong size.

If the faucet still drips after a new part

A worn valve seat is the usual reason. On a compression faucet, a seat that is pitted lets water slip past a new washer. Some seats can be resurfaced with a tool, and others should be replaced. If you cannot remove the seat, replacing the whole faucet is the practical answer.

Scale and bathroom faucets in South Plainfield

Middlesex Water, which serves portions of South Plainfield, reported average hardness of 157 ppm in its 2025 report. Scale builds inside cartridges and on aerators and can seize parts, so some bathroom faucets in the borough are harder to repair than they look.

If the handle will not turn or the retaining nut is frozen with mineral buildup, soaking it in vinegar can help. If it does not, stop before something breaks and call a plumber.

When to replace the faucet

An old faucet with a worn finish, corrosion or parts that are no longer made is better replaced. A new faucet usually costs less than repeated repairs, and a plumber can install a faucet you supply or recommend one that fits your sink.

While the sink is open, check the shutoff valves. A valve that weeps or will not close should be replaced at the same time, since it will be the first thing you need in an emergency. Our plumbing installation page covers valves and fixtures.

Other causes of a wet sink cabinet

A drip does not always come from the faucet. Supply lines, shutoff valves and the drain trap can all leak, and condensation on a cold pipe can mimic a leak. Dry everything with a towel, put a sheet of paper under the pipes and see where it gets wet first.

If the cabinet is persistently damp with no clear source, a plumber can find it. If the water bill is also high, see leak detection. We serve Edison and Scotch Plains as well.

Choosing the right replacement part

Take the old cartridge or washer with you when you shop. Brands use different sizes and shapes, and a part that looks close can leak. Write down the faucet brand and model number from the underside or the box, and take a photo of the assembly before you disassemble it.

If the faucet is a well-known brand, the manufacturer's repair kit is usually the best match. Generic kits can work for common designs, but a poor fit leads to drips. If you cannot identify the faucet, a plumber can match it or recommend a replacement.

Preventing the next drip

Clean the aerator every few months to keep scale from building up, and avoid forcing the handle past its stop. Overtightening a handle compresses seals and wears them faster. If you notice a stiff handle or a weak flow, deal with it early before it becomes a drip.

Every few years, turn the shutoff valves under the sink fully closed and open again to keep them moving. A valve that has never been turned tends to seize, and you will want it working on the day you need it.

Standards note

The EPA WaterSense standard for bathroom faucets is a flow of 1.5 gallons per minute or less. If your faucet needs replacing anyway, a labeled model is a simple way to use less water.
