Free Ways to Check for a Water Leak in South Plainfield, NJ

You can check for many leaks yourself in an afternoon. Turn off every fixture, read your water meter twice about fifteen minutes apart and see whether it moved. Test toilets with dye. Look under sinks and around the water heater for dampness.

If the meter moves with everything off, you have a leak somewhere. This guide walks through each free check and explains when it is time to call a plumber for leak detection.

Start with the water meter

The meter is the most honest tool you own. Turn off every faucet, the washer, the dishwasher and the ice maker. Do not flush any toilets. Find the meter, usually in the basement or a utility area, and look for a small triangle or dial that spins when water flows.

If the triangle or the lowest dial turns with everything off, water is escaping. Note the reading, wait fifteen minutes with no water use and read it again. Any change is a leak. A faster spin means a bigger leak.

Is the leak inside or outside the house?

Close the main shutoff valve inside the house and watch the meter. If it keeps moving, the leak is between the meter and the shutoff, so on the service line. If it stops, the leak is inside the house. That one test tells you whether you have a plumbing problem or a service line problem.

Check your toilets with dye

Toilets are the most common source of hidden leaks. Put a few drops of food coloring in the tank and do not flush. If color appears in the bowl within fifteen minutes, the flapper is leaking. A toilet can lose many gallons a day this way without any sound.

Look for signs around the house

Damp cabinet floors

Check under every sink for moisture and swollen wood.

Stained ceilings

Water travels, so the stain may not be under the leak.

Warm floor spots

This can mean a hot water line leaking under a slab.

Musty smell

Hidden moisture often smells before it shows.

Check the water heater, the washing machine hoses and the laundry sink. Look at outside faucets and any irrigation system. Walk the yard after a dry spell and look for a patch of green grass or damp soil in line with the main.

What a water bill tells you in South Plainfield

Middlesex Water serves portions of South Plainfield, and your bill shows usage in gallons or cubic feet. Compare it to the same month last year. A rise with no change in habits is a clue.

Ask your utility how it reads the meter and whether it offers leak alerts. If your address is served by a different water company, the same advice applies, and the bill is where to find the supplier's name and contact.

What the free checks cannot tell you

Free checks tell you whether you have a leak and roughly where. They do not tell you the exact location. A leak in a wall, under a slab or in a buried line is only pinpointed with thermal imaging, acoustic equipment or a pressure test.

The advantage of knowing it is a leak first is that you can describe it clearly, and a plumber can skip the early steps. Mention your meter findings and the toilet test results when you call.

What to do once you find a leak

If water is actively flowing, shut off the main valve. For a running toilet, replace the flapper. For a leaking supply line, shut the fixture valve and call. If the leak is hidden, take notes and photos and call a plumber. The earlier you find a leak, the less structural damage and the smaller the bill.

Using your bill and your meter together

Your bill shows a trend, and the meter shows the present. A bill that rose with no change in habits tells you to look. A meter test tells you whether water is moving right now. Use both. If the bill is higher but the meter is still, the cause may be seasonal use, such as filling a pool or watering a lawn.

Keep a short log of readings. Write the date and the meter number each week for a month. A steady increase that does not match your use is the clearest sign of a slow leak, and the log gives a plumber a head start.

Standards note

According to the EPA WaterSense program, household leaks waste more than 9,300 gallons of water every year on average, and nine percent of homes have leaks that waste 50 gallons or more per day. A meter test takes fifteen minutes and is worth doing.
