What counts as a commercial emergency

A restroom that cannot be used, a main that is backing up into a kitchen or a pipe flooding a stockroom all qualify. Shut off the water at the main if you can reach it, move stock and electronics out of the wet area and call the 24/7 line.

Tell the dispatcher your business type, the building size and any access limits such as alarm codes, loading docks or a landlord who controls the shutoff. That lets the plumber bring the right tools.

Common commercial calls

Restaurants and food shops need grease line cleaning, floor drain clearing and fast fixes to prep sinks and dishwashers. Offices and retail spaces most often call about restroom fixtures, flushometers and water heaters. Light industrial and warehouse space brings larger drains and service lines.

Along Stelton Road, New Durham Road and Park Avenue, many South Plainfield businesses share buildings with a landlord. Ask who owns the shutoff and the water heater before an emergency, because it shortens the call.

Maintenance that avoids emergencies

A scheduled jetting visit, a backflow test where required and a yearly look at the heater and shutoffs reduce surprise calls. A written list of shutoff locations posted near the front office saves minutes at a time when minutes cost money.

Our drain cleaning and installation pages cover the work in more detail. Commercial properties with a sump pit or floor pump should also check it before storm season.

Code and standards note

New Jersey's plumbing subcode, N.J.A.C. 5:23-3.15, adds a state rule for public restrooms: lavatory outlet temperature must be limited to 110 degrees Fahrenheit. That standard is one reason a restroom heater and its mixing valve deserve a yearly check in a shop or restaurant.

What to do until the plumber arrives

  • Shut off the water at the affected fixture or the main valve.
  • Keep staff and customers away from wet floors and anything electrical.
  • Stop using drains that are backing up, including floor drains in the kitchen.
  • Photograph the damage and any stock that got wet for your insurer.
  • Tell the dispatcher about access limits such as alarm codes, a locked mechanical room or a landlord contact.

What to have ready when you call

A fast response is easier when the dispatcher knows the address, the type of business, what is failing and whether customers or staff are in the space. Mention any access details such as a locked mechanical room, a landlord contact, alarm codes or loading dock hours.

For a restaurant, say whether the kitchen is still operating and whether the problem involves a grease trap, a floor drain or a fixture. For an office or shop, say whether restrooms are still usable. That lets the plumber prioritize the part that is stopping business and bring the parts for it.
