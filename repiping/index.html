Repiping in South Plainfield, NJ

When old pipes leak repeatedly, run rusty or deliver weak pressure, patching stops being economical. Repiping replaces the failing supply lines with new pipe so the house has a fresh start.

Signs it is time to repipe

Low water pressure

Galvanized pipe corrodes inside and narrows over decades, which cuts flow.

Brown or rusty water

Corroding steel releases rust, especially after the water sits unused.

Repeated pinhole leaks

One leak is a repair. A third in a year points at the whole line.

Visible corrosion at joints

Green or white crust and flaking pipe mean the metal is failing.

Houses built before the 1960s commonly have galvanized steel supply lines. By now many of those have reached the end of their service life, and a house with its original lines should be checked even if it is not leaking yet.

Copper or PEX

Copper is durable and long-lived and resists bacteria and UV. It costs more and takes more labor. PEX is flexible plastic, is faster to install and can often be fished through walls with fewer openings. It does not corrode and handles freezing better, though it should not be exposed to sunlight.

A plumber will recommend one for your house based on layout and budget. Many repipes combine both, with copper at the main and PEX in the branches.

Water quality and the lines

Middlesex Water, which serves portions of South Plainfield, reports a pH of 7.7 and a 90th percentile lead level of 1 ppb in its 2025 report. Utilities adjust water to reduce corrosion in the lines, but that cannot undo decades of buildup inside old galvanized pipe.

If the discoloration appears only at certain taps, a local blockage may be the cause. If every tap shows it, the lines are suspect. Leak detection can find a leak in a line that is still repairable.

Code and standards note

New Jersey's plumbing subcode, N.J.A.C. 5:23-3.15, adopts the National Standard Plumbing Code, 2021 edition, published by the International Association of Plumbing and Mechanical Officials. The work must be done by a licensed master plumber or an employee of a licensed contractor under N.J.S.A. 45:14C-1, and permit rules vary, so ask the South Plainfield construction office.

What to do if a pipe fails before the repipe

  • Close the main water valve, usually near the meter.
  • Open a low faucet to drain the lines, then keep clear of wet outlets and the panel.
  • Photograph the leak and the damage for your insurer.
  • Call the 24/7 line and describe where the leak is.
  • If you see rusty or brown water, run a cold tap for a few minutes and tell the plumber what you saw.

Repipe assessments are available in Plainfield and across the area, and new valves and fixtures during the project fall under plumbing installation.

What happens during a repipe

The plumber maps the existing lines, plans new runs through the house and sets up in the order that keeps water off for the shortest time. New lines are installed in open framing where possible and fished through walls where not. Each line is pressure tested before drywall is touched.

Small access holes are cut in walls and ceilings, then patched. Patching and painting can be included or left to a separate contractor, and you should ask which at the price stage. Most households are without water for part of a day at a time and are back to normal in a few days.

Spot repairs versus whole-house work

A single pinhole leak in an otherwise sound line is a spot repair. Several leaks within a short time, low pressure throughout the house and discolored water together point to a systemic problem. A plumber can show you the inside of a cut section, which is often convincing on its own.

Partial repipes, such as replacing a bathroom's lines or one floor, are a middle path. They spread the cost and deal with the worst pipe first, though each opening is a chance to see what the rest looks like.
